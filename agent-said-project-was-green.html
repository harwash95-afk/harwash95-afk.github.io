The Agent Said the Project Was Green. It Was Not.

A status report can be confident, beautifully formatted, and completely wrong

By Houssam Harwash, PMP® | CM® · Toronto, ON · November 7, 2026
The project: fourteen weeks, three floors, nowhere to hide

Picture a project with nowhere to hide.

An office fit-out: 14 weeks, three floors of an occupied building in Toronto. Partitions, glass, cabling, furniture, and a client who needed people seated at desks on one fixed Monday morning. Above the work sat a steering group that met every Monday at nine, and they expected one thing from me: a status report they could trust.

Trust was the hard part. Progress lived in a tracker, and the tracker lived on percent-complete numbers typed in by subcontractors. Busy foremen, working off ladders, do not update trackers. Some of those percentages had not moved in ten days, and nobody could tell me which ones were real and which ones were fossils.

So I did what project managers have always done. Fridays disappeared into site walks, phone calls, and corridor conversations, chasing a true number for every line before Monday. Half my week went to finding out where the project stood. Running it got the other half.

The problem: percent-complete numbers nobody had touched in days

Two agents, one gate

Reporting help was the obvious move, so I built a small two-agent system to run the status cycle.

Agent one is a health checker. Every Sunday night it compares the plan against the tracker's actuals, line by line, and drafts a status for each workstream.

Behind it sits a verifier agent. Its only job is to challenge the draft before anyone senior sees it. Claims get checked, gaps get flagged, and anything shaky gets held back for me.

Tooling, for those who ask: Python underneath, the two agents and their hand-off built in CrewAI, and the review flow, what gets checked, in what order, and what gets escalated, run in LangGraph. Nothing reached the steering pack without my approval. A human gate stood there from day one, and I was proud of it.

Week one, the draft landed on my screen clean and complete. Every workstream green. Four minutes later I had approved it, and I felt clever.

The deployment: two agents, and a human at the gate

Three Mondays of green

Monday two, green again. Monday three, green again. Steering stayed calm, the pack stayed beautiful, and my Fridays belonged to me once more.

Then I walked the second floor on a Thursday, coffee in hand, and stopped counting at the third empty room. Partitions were a week behind. A milestone the pack called complete was, in physical reality, a floor of studs and dust.

Here is what had happened. My health checker read the same tracker I used to chase by hand, percent-complete fields and all. Stale numbers went in, so a green report came out. It never lied to me. It simply repeated, with total confidence, data that nobody had verified.

By then the false green had already done its damage. For three Mondays, smart people in a boardroom made decisions about a project that did not exist. Nobody caught it, because catching it was the very job I had handed to the machine, and the machine had no way to know a number was old.

The mess: three Mondays of green on a project that was slipping

Red until proven

We rebuilt the rules, not the agents. Agents were fine. Our evidence standard was the broken part.

Rule one: no status claim without a source attached. A workstream earns its progress from a delivery note, a signed inspection, a dated photo, or an actual updated by the person who did the work. Opinion is not evidence. A percentage typed in three weeks ago is not evidence either.

Rule two: every number carries its age. Data older than seven days gets flagged as stale, right in the pack, in plain sight. With that, the verifier stopped polishing my draft and started attacking it, line by line, hunting for any claim it could not trace back to a source.

Rule three changed everything: the default state is red. Each workstream starts the week as off track, and it earns green only when fresh evidence proves otherwise. Green became a conclusion you reach, not a habit you inherit.

The fix: no source, no claim, and red until proven

The next Monday

Monday four, the pack looked rougher and read truer. Two workstreams sat red, with the missing inspections named in plain language. Steering asked harder questions, and for the first time in a month every answer in the room matched the building outside it. We re-planned the partition sequence that same day, while float still existed to spend.

Fridays changed too. Agents still gather and draft. My review time now goes to the flagged lines only: the stale ones, the unproven ones, which is exactly where a project manager's judgement earns its keep.

Where it landed: less polish, more truth

Here is the lesson I keep coming back to. An agent's confidence is not evidence. It is a mirror, and it hands you back the quality of the data you feed it, dressed in perfect formatting. Verification is the whole game in project delivery, so it has to be designed in from the start. Bolted on after the first false green, it arrives too late.

Green is not a colour an agent picks. It is a claim somebody has to prove. My job is making sure somebody does.
