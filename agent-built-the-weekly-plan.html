I Let an Agent Build the Weekly Plan. It Booked the Same Crew Twice.

A planning problem I first met on paper in Saudi Arabia, rebuilt this year at my own desk, and the rules that keep a weekly plan honest

By Houssam Harwash, Economist | PMP® | CM® · Toronto, ON · November 7, 2026

Last time, the agents reported the past. In the first piece, a small crew read the trackers, chased the updates, and assembled the Friday pack. This time I gave them the future. I let an agent build the weekly plan, the document every other promise hangs on.

The problem underneath is older than any of that. I first met it during my years in Saudi Arabia, running projects and planning for an oil and gas services company. The plan looked healthy every Monday morning. The signed records told a different story.

Then: paper, signatures, and polite numbers

The office ran the planning cycle for a portfolio of client service contracts: annual and quarterly plans, progress tracking across every contract, and the tenders and purchase orders that fed the work. Once a week, everything rolled into one plan, and the week was committed against it.

Progress came in as reports from contract supervisors. Updating a progress figure took effort, and effort was scarce, so numbers were copied forward from the week before. Read across the portfolio, claimed progress sat at 78 percent. It looked like a business in good health. Nobody was falsifying anything. Copying forward was faster, and the week never waited.

Then we checked by hand. Signed work completion records. Delivery notes. Approved timesheets. Verified completions sat near 61 percent. Seventeen points of the plan were fiction, and crews were being scheduled, and clients promised, against work that had never happened.

Claimed 78 percent. Verified 61. The gap was the story.

The discipline that came out of those years is simple to say and slow to do. Every figure earns its place with a document behind it. No signature, no progress. It worked. It also ate hours that planning should have spent looking forward.

Now: the same cycle, rebuilt in Toronto

This year, at my own desk in Toronto, I rebuilt that weekly cycle as a working build. The portfolio is modeled on the one I used to run: 9 active contracts, roughly 130 open work items in the rolling weekly plan. Same shape of problem, same question underneath. Can the cycle run without inheriting its oldest bad habit? I wanted to know whether a discipline learned by hand could be taught to a machine.

Two agents, built with CrewAI. The Planner drafts the weekly plan from actuals and open items, nothing else. The Checker tests the draft against crew capacity and the source document behind every line. The flow runs in LangGraph: draft, check, revise, and it stops at a human approval gate before the plan locks. Python holds it together. Roles, inputs, outputs, and acceptance criteria were written down before anything ran.

Now: two agents draft and check, a human decides

The mess: a perfect plan that could not happen

Week one, the Planner did exactly what the old reports used to do. It rolled last week's unfinished items forward as if they were fresh. Nothing expired. Nothing aged. Every optimistic number it was fed came back wearing a new date.

At the gate, I cross-checked the draft against the signed completion records and delivery notes, the old habit, and found it. One crew, the same named crew, was planned onto two contracts in two different cities in the same week. The draft also showed spare capacity that did not exist, because rolled items carried hours nobody had re-estimated. The plan was clean, balanced, and impossible.

The mess: one crew, two cities, one week

The agent had not invented a new failure. It had automated the old one, at machine speed.

The fix: the old discipline, written as rules

So the rules from the paper years went in as hard constraints. Plan only from verified actuals. Every planned line carries its source document: a signed completion record, a delivery note, an approved timesheet. No document, no line.

Unfinished items expire after 7 days. They never roll forward silently; they return to a human for a decision: finish, replan, or drop. A capacity check against named crews runs before the plan can lock, so one crew can never again be in two cities at once. The agent proposes. The planner disposes.

The fix: expiry first, capacity second, lock last

Where it landed

Within a month, weekly forecast error fell from about 26 percent to about 7 percent. The plan the Checker approved was smaller than the plan the Planner wanted to write. It was also, for the first time in the build, possible. Monday stopped being an argument about arithmetic and became an argument about trade-offs, which is what a planning meeting is for.

One month in: the weekly forecast, before and after

A plan built on unverified data is fiction with good formatting. Reporting is where bad data gets noticed. Planning is where it does its damage first, because every promise downstream is priced on it.

The discipline did not change in a decade. Documents over claims. Verification before commitment. What changed is who does the checking, and how tired they get. Agents do not get tired. That is the whole case for them, and the whole reason they still need a gate.
